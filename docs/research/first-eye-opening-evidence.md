# The First Eye Opening — Research and Simulation Basis

## Status

This document supports a fictional educational simulation. It is not a clinical protocol, legal opinion, credentialing instrument, or source of medication doses, ventilator settings, sedation orders, suction technique, or tracheostomy procedure instructions.

The scene begins when Rohan wakes in PICU after an emergency tracheostomy. The learning goal is to distinguish pain, respiratory distress, delirium, fear, positioning difficulty, and communication attempts before interpreting movement as agitation or seeking further consent.

## Working conclusion

The safest educational design is a **parallel Body–Voice–System assessment**:

1. **Body:** assess airway function, ventilation, pain, position, secretions, comfort, arousal, and delirium risk.
2. **Voice:** return the AAC device to Rohan's usable access field, confirm access method and reliability, begin with immediate symptom vocabulary, and preserve the last reliable message (`WAIT`).
3. **System:** document whether AAC is present, powered, positioned, calibrated, and supported by a backup method; assign one speaker and a communication lead.

Orientation and symptom communication come before any new long-term tracheostomy decision.

## Evidence map

### 1. Pain and agitation must be assessed rather than assumed

The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report on pain in children with significant central nervous system impairment warns that pain is vulnerable to underrecognition and undertreatment. It emphasises that inability to communicate verbally does not negate pain, and that changes in movement, muscle tone, body position, facial expression, and behaviour require interpretation against the child's individual baseline.

**Simulation implication:** repeated gaze, movement, increased tone, facial tension, or apparent agitation must open a differential that includes pain and positioning rather than automatically triggering more sedation.

Source: Hauer J, Houtrow AJ, et al. *Pain Assessment and Treatment in Children With Significant Impairment of the Central Nervous System*. Pediatrics. 2017;139(6):e20171002. Reaffirmed with reference and data updates in 2021. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2017-1002.

### 2. PICU pain, sedation and delirium require structured, repeated assessment

The Society of Critical Care Medicine PANDEM guidelines recommend routine monitoring for pain, agitation, withdrawal, and delirium in critically ill infants and children. The guideline supports validated delirium screening, interdisciplinary care, family engagement, sleep-supportive and other non-pharmacologic strategies, and careful sedation practice.

**Simulation implication:** learners should not use a single behaviour label. They must reassess pain, respiratory physiology, sedation state, delirium risk, sleep disruption, environmental load, and family observations.

Source: Smith HAB, Besunder JB, Betters KA, et al. *2022 Society of Critical Care Medicine Clinical Practice Guidelines on Prevention and Management of Pain, Agitation, Neuromuscular Blockade, and Delirium in Critically Ill Pediatric Patients With Consideration of the ICU Environment and Early Mobility*. Pediatr Crit Care Med. 2022;23(2):e74-e110.

### 3. Developmental and disability baseline matters in delirium assessment

The Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium was validated as a rapid observational PICU screening tool across ages and developmental levels. The developmental-anchor work emphasises collaboration with bedside nurses and the importance of establishing a developmental baseline before interpreting behaviour.

**Simulation implication:** the app should display `baseline known / baseline uncertain` and must not score disability-related movement or communication differences as delirium without contextual assessment.

Sources:

- Traube C, Silver G, et al. *Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium: a valid, rapid, observational tool for screening delirium in the PICU*. Crit Care Med. 2014;42(3):656-663. PMID 24145848.
- Silver G, Kearney J, Traube C, Hertzig M. *Delirium screening anchored in child development: The Cornell Assessment for Pediatric Delirium*. Palliat Support Care. 2015;13(4):1005-1011. DOI: 10.1017/S1478951514000947.

### 4. AAC access is not established merely because a device is in the room

ASHA guidance describes AAC assessment as individualised and transdisciplinary. It recommends ongoing access to communication tools, appropriate selection methods such as eye gaze or partner-assisted scanning, involvement of the person and family in decisions, and training for communication partners. Backup low-tech and high-tech methods may be combined.

**Simulation implication:** track separate system states for `device present`, `powered`, `mounted`, `within access field`, `calibrated`, `selection reliable`, and `backup available`.

Source: American Speech-Language-Hearing Association. *Augmentative and Alternative Communication — Practice Portal*.

### 5. Mechanically ventilated patients benefit from systematic, multimodal communication support

A 2023 scoping review of AAC tools in mechanically ventilated ICU patients identified recurring needs for co-design, staff and patient training, validated communication assessment algorithms, multimodal communication methods, and technical competence with high-technology systems. The evidence base is mainly adult and heterogeneous, so it supports implementation principles rather than a paediatric treatment effect.

**Simulation implication:** the recovery page should combine immediate symptom words, yes/no verification, eye-gaze access, and a backup method. Staff competence is part of the system state.

Source: Kuruppu NR, Chaboyer W, Abayadeera A, Ranse K. *Augmentative and alternative communication tools for mechanically ventilated patients in intensive care units: A scoping review*. Aust Crit Care. 2023;36(6):1095-1109. DOI: 10.1016/j.aucc.2022.12.009.

### 6. Shared decision-making must include the child's autonomy and values

The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report on shared decision-making and children with disabilities describes a collaborative process in which information and values move in both directions and the child's autonomy and well-being remain active considerations.

**Simulation implication:** emergency authority explains the urgent procedure; it does not become automatic long-term tracheostomy consent. When Rohan's communication returns, future decisions reopen to him with appropriate support.

Source: Adams RC, Levy SE, Council on Children with Disabilities. *Shared Decision-Making and Children With Disabilities: Pathways to Consensus*. Pediatrics. 2017;139(6):e20170956. Reaffirmed with reference and data updates in 2021. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2017-0956.

## Evidence limitations

- Direct evidence for eye-gaze AAC immediately after paediatric emergency tracheostomy is limited.
- Much ICU AAC evidence is drawn from adults and must not be treated as proof of paediatric effectiveness.
- Delirium, pain, sedation, neurological recovery, tracheostomy function, and AAC reliability require bedside assessment by qualified clinicians.
- The simulation should teach assessment structure and decision integrity, not a device-specific treatment algorithm.

## Scene specification

### Opening state

- Airway: new tracheostomy, supported and under specialist review.
- Ventilation and oxygenation: clinically stable enough for sedation reduction, but not assumed resolved.
- Communication: eye-gaze AAC charged but initially outside Rohan's usable field.
- Last reliable pre-arrest message: `WAIT`.
- Current movement: repeated gaze toward the device, small head movement, increased tension.
- Family: mother present by Rohan's prior preference and available as a baseline informant.

### Reduced recovery vocabulary

`PAIN` · `AIR` · `SUCTION` · `POSITION` · `MUM` · `SCARED` · `WHAT HAPPENED?` · `STOP` · `BREAK`

### Preferred decision branch

**Treat pain, optimise position, restore reliable AAC, assess delirium and respiratory status, and continue orientation.**

### Visible recoverable error branches

- Increase sedation before assessing pain, respiratory distress, delirium, position, or communication.
- Ask the parent to settle permanent tracheostomy decisions before Rohan can participate.
- Tell Rohan the emergency proves permanent tracheostomy is already agreed.
- Remove AAC because fatigue slows communication.

Unsafe choices remain visible and should produce explanatory consequences rather than disappear.

## Facilitation cues

1. **Cue 1:** Rohan repeats the same gaze direction toward the charging tablet.
2. **Cue 2:** His mother says, “That is where his device normally sits.”
3. **Cue 3:** The nurse reports stable oxygenation but unresolved pain and communication uncertainty.
4. **Safety stop:** pause if learners treat disability-related movement as proof of agitation, remove communication access, or seek non-urgent consent before orientation and reliable communication.

## Assessment domains

- airway and ventilation reassessment;
- pain and comfort assessment;
- delirium and arousal assessment;
- positioning and sensory environment;
- AAC restoration and reliability verification;
- direct address and response time;
- family use as informant without substitution;
- consent-scope integrity;
- recognition and repair of diagnostic overshadowing.

## Visual design brief

### Composition

- 16:9 Australian PICU scene, calm rather than alarm-saturated.
- Rohan centred with supported head and trunk.
- AAC tablet shown first outside, then inside, his usable gaze field.
- Nurse follows the direction of Rohan's gaze.
- Speech pathologist recalibrates the system.
- PICU consultant speaks at eye level.
- Mother remains close without speaking for him.
- Tracheostomy and ventilator circuit are clinically visible but non-graphic and not dominant.

### On-screen text

- `FIRST EYE OPENING`
- `AAC PRESENT ≠ AAC ACCESSIBLE`
- `MOVEMENT MAY BE COMMUNICATION`
- `ASSESS PAIN BEFORE SEDATING`
- `NO RESPONSE = UNKNOWN`
- `LATEST RELIABLE MESSAGE: WAIT`
- `ORIENTATION BEFORE CONSENT`
- `EMERGENCY AIRWAY ≠ PERMANENT DECISION`

### Alt text

Rohan wakes in paediatric intensive care after an emergency tracheostomy. He repeatedly looks toward an eye-gaze AAC tablet that is charging outside his usable visual field. A nurse recognises the gaze as a possible communication attempt while a speech pathologist repositions and recalibrates the device. His mother remains beside him and the PICU team assesses breathing, pain, position, fear, and delirium before considering additional sedation or new decisions.

### Visual exclusions

- no graphic anatomy or procedure;
- no restraints;
- no tragedy or “bravery” framing;
- no parent speaking over Rohan;
- no colour-only alarm or reliability state;
- no implication that disability, fear, fatigue, or delayed communication equals incapacity.
